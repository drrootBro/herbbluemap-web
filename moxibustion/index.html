Moxibustion: What It Is, Benefits, Breech Babies and Safety

Moxibustion warms acupuncture points by burning moxa — dried, aged mugwort (ai ye) — close to the skin. In Chinese medicine it is the treatment of choice when a problem is cold, weak or chronic, and it is the second half of the Chinese word for acupuncture itself: zhen jiu, "needle and moxa".

How it is done

Most common in the US is indirect moxa: a cigar-shaped moxa stick is held an inch above the skin until the area feels pleasantly warm, or moxa is burned on top of an acupuncture needle or on a slice of ginger. Smokeless and stick-on moxa patches exist for home use.

What it is used for

Cold-type joint and back pain, menstrual cramps that improve with warmth, digestive weakness with loose stools, and fatigue. A classic home point is ST36 below the knee.

Moxibustion for breech babies

Warming the point BL67 at the outer corner of the little toenail, usually around weeks 33–35, is a traditional way to encourage a breech baby to turn. Reviews of the trials suggest it may reduce the number of babies still breech at birth, but the evidence is low-quality. Only do this with your obstetrician or midwife's agreement.

Safety

Burns are the main risk: keep the stick moving, keep it an inch away, and never use moxa on numb skin (for example with diabetic neuropathy) or on the face. Moxa smoke is strong; ventilate the room and avoid it with asthma.

Questions, answered straight

What is moxibustion good for?

Traditionally for cold-type, chronic problems: joint and back pain that improves with heat, menstrual cramps, weak digestion, and turning breech babies.

Does moxibustion work for breech babies?

Trials suggest it may help, but the evidence is low-quality. Use it only with your obstetrician's or midwife's agreement, usually around weeks 33–35.

Is moxibustion safe at home?

Indirect moxa with care is generally safe. Avoid burns, never use it on numb skin, and ventilate the room.
