Shang Han Lun for Beginners: The Handbook Behind the Formulas

Around the year 200, an epidemic swept through the clan of a Chinese physician named Zhang Zhongjing. In his preface he writes that of two hundred relatives, two-thirds died within a decade — most of them from “cold damage.” His answer was a book: the Shang Han Lun, the Treatise on Cold Damage. Eighteen centuries later it is still in print, still memorized by students, and still the source of formulas prescribed every day. This page is the short introduction. The free tool at the bottom is where the book becomes something you can touch.

Not a theory book — a field manual

Most famous medical classics explain the universe. This one behaves like a senior doctor’s notebook from a disaster zone: terse, practical, organized by what you are looking at rather than by what things mean. Each entry names a pattern — this cluster of signs, at this stage — and gives the formula for it, the dose, the decoction method, and the warnings. Theory exists, but it is kept in its place: underneath the cases, holding them up.

The six stages, in one breath each

Zhang’s organizing idea is that illness moves. An externally contracted disease walks through six stages (“conformations”), and the formula depends on which room it is currently in:

  • Taiyang 太阳 — the surface. The illness is at the gates: chills, fever, stiff neck, a floating pulse. Open the surface and show it out.
  • Yangming 阳明 — the furnace. It has gone inside and turned to heat: high fever, sweat, thirst, a body like a stove. Clear the heat, protect the fluids.
  • Shaoyang 少阳 — the hinge. Half in, half out: alternating chills and fever, bitter taste, a stuck feeling. Don’t push either way — harmonize the hinge.
  • Taiyin 太阴 — the cold kitchen. Now the illness is cold and the digestion is failing: fullness, vomiting, no thirst. Warm the center.
  • Shaoyin 少阴 — the deep reserves. The body’s fire itself is low — or heat is burning in the depths. The most delicate room in the house.
  • Jueyin 厥阴 — the last hinge. Hot and cold tangled together at the end of the line. The book’s hardest patterns, and its most famous formulas.
One cold, one formula — use the wrong one, and the illness doesn’t just stay. It gets worse, and moves deeper.

That sentence is the book’s beating heart, and the reason stage matters more than disease name. A “cold” in the Taiyang room gets Guizhi Tang; the same “cold” driven into Yangming by a wrong prescription is a different emergency with a different formula. Modern textbooks of Chinese medicine are largely commentaries on this architecture — the Shang Han Lun is the building itself.

One honest warning, in the spirit of the book itself: the tool teaches how the classical system reasons. Questions, answered straight

What is the Shang Han Lun?

The Shang Han Lun (Treatise on Cold Damage), written by Zhang Zhongjing around the year 200, is the oldest surviving clinical handbook of Chinese medicine: a pattern-by-pattern manual of formulas for externally contracted illness, organized by six stages of progression. It remains a foundational text — and a living prescription source — today.

Who was Zhang Zhongjing?

A Han-dynasty physician (c. 150–219) who wrote the book after an epidemic killed most of his extended clan. He is revered in China as the “sage of medicine,” and the formula literature of East Asia — Chinese, Japanese Kampo, Korean — descends largely from his two works, the Shang Han Lun and the Jin Gui Yao Lue.

Is the Shang Han Lun still used today?

Yes — its formulas (Guizhi Tang, Xiao Chaihu Tang and dozens more) are prescribed daily across East Asia and the West, and manufactured as standard pharmacy products in Japan’s Kampo system. Few 1,800-year-old books of any kind are still working documents; this one is.

What is the difference between Shang Han Lun medicine and modern TCM?

Modern TCM is a 20th-century standardized system built from many sources. The Shang Han Lun lineage (“classical” or jing fang medicine) works from Zhang’s text directly: six stages, pattern-first, and a compact formulary of about 260 classical prescriptions. Same ancestors, different filing system — and, practitioners of the lineage would add, different sharpness.
