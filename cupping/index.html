Cupping Therapy: Benefits, Side Effects, Marks and What to Expect

Cupping uses glass, plastic or silicone cups to create suction on the skin, lifting the tissue underneath. It is one of the oldest therapies in Chinese medicine and became famous worldwide when Olympic swimmers showed up with round purple marks in 2016.

How cupping works

Suction pulls skin and muscle upward, increasing local blood flow and stretching tight fascia. Practitioners leave cups in place for 5–15 minutes or slide them along oiled skin. In classical terms, it moves stuck qi and blood.

What do the marks mean?

The round marks are small broken capillaries under the skin, not ordinary bruises from injury. They are usually painless and fade in 3–10 days. Practitioners read darker marks as more stagnation; science has not confirmed that reading.

Benefits and evidence

Studies suggest cupping may reduce neck and back pain and muscle tension in the short term, though many trials are small. It is best seen as a hands-on therapy for tight, sore muscles, often added to an acupuncture session.

Side effects and who should avoid it

Expect marks and mild soreness. Avoid cupping on broken or inflamed skin, varicose veins, or over the belly and lower back in pregnancy, and talk to your doctor if you take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder. Fire cupping should only be done by trained practitioners because of burn risk.

Questions, answered straight

Does cupping hurt?

Usually not. It feels like a strong pulling or deep massage. Sliding cupping can feel intense over very tight muscles.

How long do cupping marks last?

Typically 3 to 10 days, depending on the person and how strong the suction was.

Is cupping safe?

Generally yes with a trained practitioner. Avoid it on damaged skin, and check with your doctor if you take blood thinners or are pregnant.
