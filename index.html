One herb at a time — or a whole system?

Western herbalism usually starts with one plant and one job: echinacea for immunity, valerian for sleep. A shelf of single tools, each with its own label.

Classical Chinese medicine starts somewhere else. It asks what state this body is in — and the first thing it reads is temperature. Patterns run hot or cold; herbs run warming or cooling. The work is to push against the pattern: cooling herbs for a heat pattern, warming herbs for a cold one. Get the direction wrong and at best nothing happens. At worst, you have poured fuel on a fire, or laid frost on snow.

That is why herbs here are almost never used alone. A classical formula is a small crew with named jobs. The Emperor does the main work. The Minister backs him up. The Assistant handles side effects and secondary problems. The Envoy guides the whole crew and keeps it pointed one way. Jun chen zuo shi — sovereign, minister, assistant, envoy.

The ginseng problem shows what the crew is for. Ginseng warms. Take too much of it and you get the classic overheating signs: a dry mouth, a parched tongue. But what if the body genuinely needs what ginseng does? The classical answer is not to abandon it. In Sheng Mai San — a formula written down centuries ago — ginseng works beside maidong (ophiopogon), a cool, moistening root whose entire assignment is to cancel the dryness, while schisandra holds the formula together. The benefit stays. The side effect gets a chaperone. That chaperone is exactly what the Assistant's job exists for.

The same thinking runs the classical approach to an ordinary cold. The old texts never ask which herb kills a virus. If the pattern is chills and no sweat — a body sealed shut — the strategy is to open the surface: fresh ginger and cinnamon twig coax out a sweat, and the illness is shown the door, out through the skin. The target was never the germ. It is the body's own machinery, adjusted until the body clears the problem itself.

Which is why one disease name earns different formulas in different people. A cold in a strong body and a cold in a depleted one are two different patterns — and it is the pattern, not the disease label, that writes the formula.
