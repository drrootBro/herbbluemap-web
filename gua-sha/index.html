Gua Sha: The Real Body Technique Behind the Face Tool

Gua sha means "scraping sha": a smooth-edged tool is stroked firmly over oiled skin until faint red speckles (the "sha") appear. Long before it became a jade face tool on social media, it was a folk and clinical remedy for neck and back stiffness, fever and heatstroke.

Body gua sha vs face gua sha

Body gua sha is firm, done on the back, neck, shoulders and limbs, and is meant to bring up sha. Face gua sha is very light, never meant to mark the skin, and works more like lymphatic massage — it may reduce puffiness for a few hours.

Benefits and evidence

Small studies report less neck and back pain and better range of motion after gua sha, and increased local blood flow in the treated area. Evidence is limited but the technique is low-risk when done properly.

Side effects and safety

Body gua sha leaves red-purple petechiae that fade in 2–4 days and can feel tender. Skip it over broken skin, rashes, varicose veins or recent injuries, and check with your doctor if you take blood thinners.

Gentle gua sha at home (neck and shoulders)

Apply oil, hold the tool at a low angle, and stroke downward from the base of the skull toward the shoulder, 10–15 strokes per line. Stop if it is painful. Pair it with the self-massage points in our Pain Point Finder.

Questions, answered straight

Does gua sha really work?

Small studies suggest it can ease neck and back pain and stiffness. Face gua sha may reduce puffiness briefly; claims of permanent face-lifting are not supported.

Is gua sha supposed to bruise?

Body gua sha is meant to bring up red speckles (sha) that fade in a few days. Face gua sha should never mark the skin.

How often can I do gua sha?

Body gua sha: wait until the marks fade, usually several days. Gentle face gua sha can be done daily.
